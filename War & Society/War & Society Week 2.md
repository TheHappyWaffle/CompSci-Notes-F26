- <u>War:</u> Organized social violence
	- Is war the product of civilization or human nature?
- <u>The Phalanx Formation:</u> A type of formation used in war composed of a solid rectangle of heavily armoured infantry men known as "Hoplites"
	- All early Greek states fought using this formation
	- The Hoplite embodied military virtue![[Pasted image 20261016145902.png|606]]
- <u>The Persian Wars (499-482 BC):</u> A series of conflicts between Persia and the states of Greece, 
	- Persia threatened survival of Greek civilization
	- Persian army reflected organization of Empire
	- Themistocles (Athenian Politician) inspired Athens to build fleet of Triremes, which was a fast moving warship powered by three tiers of oars on each side, as well as a sail
	- Athenian fleet defeated King Xeres’s navy at the Battle of Salamis (480 BC)
- <u>The Peloponnesian War (431-404 BC):</u> A war fought between Athens and Sparta, two leading states of ancient Greece.  The war ended with the defeat of Athens
	- Under Pericles’s leadership, Athens transformed Delian League (A military alliance of hundreds of Greek states lead by Athens) into empire based on Athenian sea power
	- Sparta led other Greek states against Athens
	- The war left Greek society devastated
- <u>Macedonian Army:</u> Created by Philip II of Macedonia, it reflected Macedonian society and allowed Philip to impose a centralized rule due to the overwhelming strength of the army.  After Philip's assassination in 336 B.C. the army was left to <u>Alexander The Great</u> to lead Greece against Persia

# <u>The Rise of Rome</u>
- <u>The Rise of Rome:</u> Rome was a cohesive society despite tensions between Patricians & Plebeians (lords and commoners), with a Republic governed by Senate & two chief  magistrates or Consuls
	- <u>Roman Legion:</u> An organized unit of very heavily armoured units consisting of 6000 men organized into Maniples (120 men).  Each member of the legion was known as a Legionary, who had to be a Roman citizen under the age of 45
		- The conquest of Italy was based on the development of the legions.  Some Italian states were treated as allies but were expected to provide troops
- <u>Punic Wars (264-146 BC):</u> A series of three major conflicts fought between the Roman Republic and the Carthaginian Empire.  In the first conflict against Carthage, Rome built their own navy inspired by the enemies navy.  In the second conflict, general Hannibal carried the fight to Italy where he destroyed the Roman army in several battles.  Scipio Africanus forced Hannibal to return to North Africa, where he defeated him at Battle of Zama
- Julias Caesar's Nephew became the first Roman emperor, where imperial rule resulted in a 200 year long period of peace known as "Pax Romana" 
	- Soldiers were no longer dependant on generals for land
	- Army camps became centers for roman civilization
- By mid 2nd century AD, barbarians began to assault the Roman Empire's frontiers, meanwhile the empire was suffering from economic decline and political corruption
	- Diocletian appointed co-emperor to rule in East (285 AD), Constantine created new capital at Byzantium or “Constantinople” (330 AD)
	- Dissolution of the Army allowed for the collapse of the Western Roman Empire in 5th century

# <u>The Dark Ages</u>
- <u>The Franks:</u> A confederation of Germanic tribes who went on to establish the most powerful early medieval kingdom in Western Europe.  <u>Clovis the First</u> later went on to unite all of the Frankish tribes under one ruler 
- <u>Comitatus:</u> A personal following of warriors bound to a leader by loyalty and reciprocal obligations.
- <u>Charlemagne (Charles the Great, c. 742–814):</u> King of the Franks who expanded Frankish territory across much of Western and Central Europe.  His rule connected military power, Christian religion, and imperial government
- <u>Carolingian Empire:</u> The empire ruled by Charlemagne and his Carolingian successors.
- <u>Stirrup:</u> A device that supports a rider's feet while mounted on a horse
	- Helped mounted warriors use weapons such as lances more effectively
- <u>Charles Martel (c. 688–741):</u> A Frankish political and military leader who strengthened Frankish power, he distributed land and revenues, including church lands, to secure the support of military followers
- <u>Christendom:</u> The community of Christian peoples and territories, political leaders and Church authorities often presented the defence or expansion of Christianity as a religious duty.
- <u>General levy:</u> A broad call for people or eligible subjects to provide military service.
- <u>Christendom:</u> The medieval Christian community and the lands associated with it.
- <u>Holy Roman Empire:</u> An empire that sought to preserve the Roman imperial legacy and represent Christian political unity in Western and Central Europe
- <u>Viking invasions:</u> Raids and invasions from Scandinavia that threatened settlements and kingdoms across Europe.
- <u>Magyars:</u> A people from the Eurasian steppe who raided parts of Central and Western Europe during the ninth and tenth centuries.

# <u>Feudalism and the Rise of Cavalry</u>
- <u>Feudalism:</u> A system of political and social relationships based on landholding, loyalty, protection, and service.
- <u>Fief:</u> Land or rights granted by a lord to a vassal in exchange for obligations, often military service.
- <u>Vassal:</u> A person who owed loyalty and service to a lord, often in return for a fief.
- <u>Serfdom:</u> A condition in which peasants were bound to a lord's estate and faced restrictions on their freedom.
- <u>Cavalry:</u> Soldiers who fight on horseback, valued for their mobility and ability to charge enemy forces.
- <u>Infantry:</u> Soldiers who fight on foot.
- <u>Vegetius's On Military Matters:</u> A Roman military treatise discussing military organization, training, and discipline.
- <u>Battle of Hastings (1066):</u> William of Normandy defeated King Harold II of England, allowing William to conquer England.
- <u>Peace of God:</u> A Church movement intended to protect vulnerable people and property from violence.
- <u>Truce of God:</u> A Church movement that attempted to restrict when fighting could take place.
- <u>Crossbow prohibition (1139):</u> The Second Lateran Council condemned the use of crossbows and bows against Christians, reflecting the Church's attempts to limit certain forms of warfare.
- <u>Chivalry:</u> A code of conduct associated with knights, emphasizing honour, courage, loyalty, and generosity.
- <u>Tournaments:</u> Organized martial contests in which knights demonstrated their combat skills and trained for warfare.

# <u>Wars of the Medieval Monarchies</u>
- <u>The Crusades:</u> Religious military campaigns undertaken by Western European Christians, particularly to capture or defend territories in the eastern Mediterranean.
- <u>First Crusade (1095–1099):</u> A campaign called by Pope Urban II that resulted in the capture of Jerusalem in 1099.
- <u>Siege warfare:</u> Military operations intended to capture fortified places by attacking their defences or cutting off supplies.
- <u>Feudal levy:</u> Soldiers raised through existing military obligations to a lord or ruler.
- <u>National monarchies:</u> Kingdoms in which kings increasingly centralized political authority and strengthened control over their territories.
- <u>Mercenaries:</u> Soldiers hired to fight in exchange for payment.
- <u>Hundred Years' War (1337–1453):</u> A series of conflicts between England and France over territorial disputes and claims to the French throne.
- <u>Edward I (1239–1307):</u> English king associated with military campaigns and the development of royal military organization.
- <u>Longbow:</u> A powerful bow used by English and Welsh archers.
    - Allowed trained archers to fire volleys of arrows that could disrupt infantry and cavalry.
- <u>Battle of Crécy (1346):</u> English victory during the Hundred Years' War that demonstrated the effectiveness of longbowmen.
- <u>Battle of Poitiers (1356):</u> English victory in which the French king, John II, was captured.
- <u>Bertrand du Guesclin:</u> French military commander who used harassment, ambushes, and siege warfare to weaken English forces.
- <u>Battle of Agincourt (1415):</u> English victory under Henry V, where archery and difficult battlefield conditions helped defeat a larger French force.

# <u>Breakdown of Feudal Warfare</u>
- <u>Joan of Arc (c. 1412–1431):</u> French heroine who helped inspire resistance against English domination during the Hundred Years' War.
    - Helped relieve the siege of Orléans in 1429.
- <u>Charles VII (1403–1461):</u> French king who strengthened royal military organization and developed a more regular standing army.
- <u>Standing army:</u> A permanent military force maintained by a government rather than assembled only for individual campaigns.
- <u>Professional army:</u> A force of soldiers organized and maintained through recruitment, payment, training, and administration.
- <u>State centralization:</u> The process by which monarchs strengthened their authority and reduced their dependence on local feudal lords.
- <u>Swiss pikemen:</u> Infantry soldiers who fought in disciplined formations armed with long pikes.
- <u>Pike:</u> A long spear-like weapon used to keep enemy soldiers, particularly cavalry, at a distance.
- <u>Battle of Laupen (1339):</u> A Swiss regional victory associated with the effectiveness of organized infantry.
- <u>Battle of Castillon (1453):</u> A French victory conventionally considered the end of the Hundred Years' War.

# <u>The Fourth Crusade</u>
- <u>Fourth Crusade (1202–1204):</u> A crusade that was diverted from its original objectives and ended with the capture of Constantinople by Western crusaders.
- <u>Constantinople:</u> The capital of the Byzantine Empire and a major centre of trade, political power, and Eastern Orthodox Christianity.
- <u>Geoffrey de Villehardouin:</u> A French nobleman and participant in the Fourth Crusade who wrote an account of the expedition and the fall of Constantinople.
- <u>Fall of Constantinople (1204):</u> The capture and sack of the Byzantine capital by Western crusaders, deepening tensions between Western Catholic and Eastern Orthodox Christians.
