## Motives & Methods of the New Warfare

- **Wars of Religion:** After dynastic struggles between France and the <u>Habsburgs</u> ended in 1559, new wars spread throughout Europe.
  - Religious wars became more ruthless and virtually unlimited.
  - **<u>French Wars of Religion</u> (1562–1598):** Civil wars fought in France.
  - **<u>Dutch Revolt</u> (1568–1609):** Revolt of the Netherlands against <u>Philip II</u> of Spain.

- **Standardization of drill, tactics, and weapons:**
  - Regiments developed with 4–5 companies containing both pikemen and “shot” (soldiers armed with firearms).
  - The musket began replacing the arquebus.
  - Musketeers needed extensive drill to coordinate loading, firing, and reloading.

- **<u>Maurice of Nassau, Prince of Orange</u>:** Developed systematic military drill.
  - Introduced musket drill, marching in step, digging, and firing by volley.
  - Improved battlefield control, discipline, and morale.
  - Reforms helped prepare the way for professional standing armies.

- **Musket drill:** Repeated training that allowed soldiers to load, fire, and reload in coordinated formations.
- **Wheel lock:** A handgun mechanism using a revolving wheel to produce a spark and ignite gunpowder.
  - Developed at the end of the 16th century.
  - Gave cavalrymen a more practical firearm.
- **Caracole:** Cavalry tactic involving mounted soldiers approaching the enemy, firing their pistols, then wheeling away to reload.

- **New ranks and command structures:**
  - **Captain:** Commanded a company.
  - **Lieutenant:** Second-in-command within a company.
  - **Colonel:** Commanded several companies.
  - **Lieutenant-General:** Commanded an army.
  - By the end of the 16th century, kings commissioned captains, helping establish a formal officer corps.

## The Thirty Years’ War (1618–1648)

- **Origins:**
  - Began as a renewal of religious conflict in Germany.
  - Expanded into a wider European struggle between France and the <u>Habsburgs</u>.
  - Catholic France, led by <u>Cardinal Richelieu</u>, allied with Protestant German princes and the King of Sweden against the Holy Roman Emperor.
  - Although religious conflict helped trigger the war, political motives also drove the fighting.

- **<u>Gustavus Adolphus</u> and the Swedish Army:**
  - Swedish king who transformed the Swedish military.
  - Used a system of national military obligation instead of relying mainly on hired mercenaries and palace guards.
  - Created a professional standing army.

- **Military reforms under <u>Gustavus Adolphus</u>:**
  - Reduced the weight of soldiers’ weapons to improve army mobility.
  - Standardized paper cartridges to speed up firearm loading.
  - Improved infantry firepower through volley fire.
  - Reduced the number of pikemen and contributed to the abandonment of body armour.
  - Organized troops into battalions of approximately 400–500 men, combining pikes and muskets.
  - Revitalized cavalry and introduced the first dragoons.
  - Standardized artillery.
- **<u>Battle of Breitenfeld</u> (1631):** Demonstrated the effectiveness of Swedish military reforms.

## The English Civil War (1642–1649)

- **King vs. Parliament:**
  - Began in 1642 due to constitutional and religious disputes between <u>Charles I</u> and Parliament.
  - **Royalists:** Supported the king.
  - **Parliamentarians:** Fought against the king's forces.
  - <u>Prince Rupert</u> commanded Royalist cavalry.
  - <u>Oliver Cromwell</u> commanded Parliament's “Ironsides.”
- **<u>Battle of Edgehill</u> (1642):** An early battle of the English Civil War.
- **“<u>Cavaliers</u>” vs. “<u>Roundheads</u>”:** Common names for the Royalists and Parliamentarians, respectively.

- **<u>New Model Army</u>:**
  - Parliament used the wealth of London merchants and the eastern gentry to create a long-service professional army.
  - Soldiers were driven by religious enthusiasm but maintained strong discipline.
  - Defeated both Royalist forces and the Scots.
  - <u>Oliver Cromwell</u> became the first English ruler to conquer all of the British Isles.

- **Military threat to constitutional liberty:**
  - The <u>New Model Army</u> helped destroy the constitutional liberty that Parliament had originally fought to defend.
  - Under <u>Cromwell</u>, districts were governed by Major Generals.
  - The restoration of <u>Charles II</u> in 1660 became possible partly because the army had developed its own grievances.

## The Army of Louis XIV

- **Reform of the French Army:**
  - Despite crushing the <u>Fronde</u> rebellions (1648–1653), France's army needed further reform.
  - <u>Marquis de Louvois</u> created a civil administration for the army.
  - Introduced:
    - A quartermaster-general's department.
    - Standardized uniforms.
    - Verbal commands.
    - A system of military inspections.
  - These reforms improved organization and centralized control of the army.

- **<u>Jean-Baptiste Colbert</u> and the sinews of war:**
  - Improved royal finances and sought to increase the wealth of the French state.
  - Followed **mercantilism:** An economic theory emphasizing the accumulation of wealth and strengthening the state's economy.
  - War encouraged the growth of modern commercial, industrial, and financial organization.

- **New causes for international rivalry:**
  - European conquest of overseas territories was enabled by superior weapons and stronger military and political organization.
  - Colonies generated wealth that could be converted into military strength.
  - By the second half of the 17th century, commercial and colonial rivalry increasingly replaced religion as the main cause of international conflict.

## Summary

The Military Revolution involved major changes in military organization, weapons, tactics, and government. Religious wars encouraged more intense conflicts, while <u>Maurice of Nassau</u> helped standardize military drill and discipline. During the <u>Thirty Years’ War</u>, <u>Gustavus Adolphus</u> improved Swedish military effectiveness through professional soldiers, paper cartridges, volley fire, and coordinated formations. The <u>English Civil War</u> demonstrated the growing power of professional armies through the <u>New Model Army</u>, although its power also threatened constitutional liberty. Under <u>Louis XIV</u>, reforms by <u>Marquis de Louvois</u> and <u>Jean-Baptiste Colbert</u> strengthened the French military and financial systems. By the late 17th century, commercial and colonial competition was increasingly replacing religious conflict as a major cause of international rivalry.

## Medieval Sea Fights

- **The Vikings:** Viking raids on Western Europe did not represent the beginning of Western sea power.
  - Viking longships primarily supported land warfare and raids rather than controlling the sea.

- **Round ships (cogs):**
  - During the 13th century, English <u>Cinque Ports</u> supplied the king with sailing vessels.
  - Ships were originally built as merchant vessels, with a length-to-beam ratio of approximately 2:1.
  - Stern and forecastles were added to make them suitable for warfare.

- **<u>Battle of Dover</u> (1217):**
  - <u>Hubert de Burgh</u> commanded 36 English round ships against a French fleet carrying reinforcements to forces in London.
  - Fighting was primarily a mêlée, involving close-range combat.
  - Strategically, the battle demonstrated growing awareness of the potential importance of sea power.

- **Medieval naval warfare:**
  - Warships were often converted merchant vessels, so trade influenced which ships were available for war.
  - The king's representative commanded the ship, men-at-arms fought, and the boatswain and crew sailed it.
  - Naval warfare largely consisted of land warfare conducted at sea.

## Naval Warfare in the Mediterranean

- **Galley:** A warship propelled mainly by oars.
  - Mediterranean naval warfare used oared vessels resembling ancient Greek and Roman ships.
  - Italians developed galleys to protect commerce and wage war.
  - Fighting galleys carried a **spur**, which could be used to ram enemy vessels.
  - Rowers were initially freemen but later were mostly enslaved people.

- **<u>Battle of Lepanto</u> (1571):**
  - <u>Don John of Austria</u> commanded a fleet of 200 Venetian and Spanish galleys and six galleasses equipped to carry guns.
  - <u>Ali Pasha</u> commanded a Turkish fleet of 210 galleys and 40 galliots.
  - Fighting included hand-to-hand combat.
  - The Christian fleet's superior adaptation of guns to naval warfare helped secure victory.

- **Decline of galley warfare:**
  - The Christian alliance failed to fully exploit its victory, and the Turkish Empire recovered.
  - <u>Battle of Lepanto</u> marked the end of an era in Mediterranean naval warfare.
  - Three-masted sailing ships emerged in the 15th century and supported voyages of exploration.
  - When armed with cannon, sailing ships transformed the meaning and potential of sea power.

## Evolution of the Sailing Warship

- **Introduction of naval artillery:**
  - Early naval guns were small breech-loaders used to repel boarding parties.
  - <u>Henry VIII</u> of England demonstrated the value of muzzle-loading “Great Guns” capable of destroying enemy ships.
  - Heavy guns were too large for traditional fore and stern castles, leading the English to develop gun decks with gunports.
  - These changes helped distinguish purpose-built warships from merchant vessels.

- **<u>Mary Rose</u>:**
  - Warship launched by <u>Henry VIII</u> in 1513.
  - Had a greater length-to-breadth ratio and carried Great Guns on its main deck.
  - Became a prototype for the later ship-of-the-line.

- **Anglo-Spanish rivalry:**
  - <u>Francis Drake</u>'s attack on <u>Cádiz</u> in 1587 demonstrated the superiority of newer sailing warships over galleys.
  - The raid was one example of English attacks threatening Spanish trade.
  - Maritime rivalry, dynastic ambition, and religious zeal encouraged <u>Philip II</u> of Spain to plan an invasion of England.

- **<u>Spanish Armada</u> (1588):**
  - Spain had more ships, greater tonnage, and more manpower.
  - England had an advantage in guns and gunners.
  - Although there was no clear-cut tactical victory, the outcome was a major strategic victory for England.
  - The invasion failed, the Armada returned to Spain, and <u>Philip II</u>'s immediate threat to England ended.

## Rise of the Royal Navy

- **Development of the battleship:**
  - After the <u>Spanish Armada</u>, cannon fire became more important than hand-to-hand combat in naval warfare.
  - England became a leader in warship development.
  - **<u>Sovereign of the Seas</u> (1637):**
    - Carried three gun decks and 100 guns.
    - Considered the first true battleship in the lecture.
  - **Three-deckers:** Served as flagships and formed lines of battle alongside two-deckers carrying 64–80 guns.
  - **Frigates:** Smaller warships carrying 24–36 guns, used as the fleet's “eyes” for scouting and reconnaissance.

- **<u>Anglo-Dutch Wars</u> (1652–1654, 1665–1667):**
  - Conflicts contributed to the development of formal naval tactics.
  - **<u>Permanent Fighting Instructions</u> (1691):** Established the victory of advocates of the line-ahead formation over those favouring mêlée combat.
  - Standardized formations helped improve naval signalling and coordination.

- **Professionalization of the English navy:**
  - During the 17th century, England developed a more professional naval establishment.
  - **<u>Admiralty</u>:** Political body responsible for naval policy.
  - **<u>Navy Board</u>:** Professional body responsible for supplying and maintaining naval equipment and materials.
  - Effective administration and organization allowed the <u>Royal Navy</u> to support English national policy on a global scale.

- **The French challenge:**
  - <u>Jean-Baptiste Colbert</u> developed the French navy as part of mercantilist policies intended to expand trade and acquire colonies.
  - French naval architects created a first-class fleet based at <u>Brest</u>.
  - **<u>Glorious Revolution</u> (1688):** Created an opportunity for France to test its new navy.
  - **<u>Battle of Beachy Head</u> (1690):**
    - <u>Admiral Tourville</u> defeated the Anglo-Dutch fleet.
    - Failed to use control of the English Channel to prevent reinforcements from reaching Ireland.
  - **<u>Anglo-Dutch victory over France</u> (1692):**
    - The Anglo-Dutch fleet defeated the French and regained control of the English Channel.
    - Marked the beginning of a long maritime struggle between France and England.

## The “Just War” Tradition

- **Just war tradition:** Ethical and philosophical ideas about when war is justified and how warfare should be conducted.
- **<u>Christine de Pizan</u>, “Just and Unjust War” (pp. 37–43):** Reading examining the distinction between just and unjust warfare.
- **<u>Hugo Grotius</u>, “The Law of War and Peace” (pp. 45–54):** Reading concerning the legal and moral principles governing war and peace.

## Summary

Western sea power developed from medieval ships used mainly for trade and land-based warfare into specialized warships capable of controlling the sea through artillery. The <u>Battle of Dover</u> demonstrated the strategic potential of naval warfare, while the <u>Battle of Lepanto</u> showed how guns could improve a fleet's fighting effectiveness. The development of sailing warships, naval gun decks, and cannon transformed ship design. England's success against the <u>Spanish Armada</u> highlighted the importance of naval artillery, and increasingly powerful battleships and formal line-ahead tactics helped professionalize naval warfare. Meanwhile, French naval expansion under <u>Jean-Baptiste Colbert</u> contributed to growing rivalry with England. Overall, sea power increasingly depended on specialized ships, effective artillery, disciplined formations, professional administration, and the ability to protect trade and project national power overseas.
