## War in Medieval Italy

- **Italian city-states:** Cities grew wealthy through commerce and trade, allowing them to challenge feudal lords and develop their own military forces.
- **<u>Battle of Legnano</u> (1176):** The <u>Lombard League</u> used pikemen to defeat German knights, demonstrating the effectiveness of organized infantry against mounted knights.
- **Crossbow:** A mechanical weapon that fired bolts.
  - Italian cities expanded crossbow production during the 11th–13th centuries.
  - Made armoured knights more vulnerable and warfare more complicated.
  - Cities increasingly hired professional soldiers instead of relying on town militias.
- **Mercenaries:** Soldiers hired to fight in exchange for payment.
- **<u>Condottieri</u>:** Professional military commanders who led mercenary companies.
  - Often treated warfare as a business.
  - Could avoid decisive battles and prolong campaigns to maintain their income.
  - Their armies of armoured horsemen became a military anachronism as warfare changed.

## Gunpowder and Artillery

- **Gunpowder:** An explosive substance that transformed warfare through firearms and artillery.
- **Black powder:** Discovered in China around 1000 AD, according to the lecture.
- **Early guns:** Initially vase-shaped weapons that fired arrows, later replaced by tubular designs firing spherical stone projectiles.
- **Bombard:** A large early cannon used primarily in sieges.
- **Siege artillery:** Large guns designed to attack fortifications.
  - Between 1465–1477, gun-founders developed smaller guns capable of matching the damage of giant bombards.
  - Stronger barrels allowed guns to fire heavy iron balls.
  - Gunpowder was formed into granules called “corns.”
  - Carriage-mounted artillery firing 25–30-pound balls made older fortifications vulnerable.
- **<u>French Invasion of Italy</u> (1494):**
  - <u>Charles VIII</u> of France invaded Italy with siege artillery.
  - The French fought more aggressively than the mercenary armies led by <u>condottieri</u>.
  - Italian city-states struggled against the military power of centralized national monarchies.
  - Marked a turning point in Italian warfare.

## Renaissance Warfare

- **<u>Renaissance</u>:** Revival of classical knowledge and culture, particularly in Italy during the 15th and 16th centuries.
- **<u>Humanism</u>:** Intellectual movement emphasizing classical learning and the study of subjects such as history, literature, and philosophy.
- **Liberal education:** Education based on classical knowledge, intended to prepare people for citizenship and public life.
- **<u>Niccolò Machiavelli</u> (1469–1527):** Italian political thinker who emphasized military strength and preparation.
  - Wrote *<u>The Prince</u>* (1513) and *<u>The Art of War</u>* (1521).
  - Wanted Italy restored to power through unification.
  - Separated political success from conventional morality, emphasizing the importance of force.
  - Quote: “A Prince ought to have no other aim or thought than war.”
- **Gun-proof fortifications:** Fortifications redesigned to withstand cannon fire.
  - Loosely compacted earth could absorb cannon shots.
  - Defences incorporated earth ramparts, ditches, and bastions.
- **<u>Trace Italienne</u>:** Italian fortification style developed to resist artillery, using low, thick earthworks and angled bastions.

## Towards the “Military Revolution”

- **<u>Protestant Reformation</u>:** Religious movement that divided Western Christianity and challenged the authority of the Catholic Church.
  - Contributed to struggles over sovereignty and the independence of national states.
- **<u>Franco-Habsburg Struggle</u>:** Conflict between France and the <u>Habsburg dynasty</u> over territory and dominance, particularly in Italy.
- **<u>Charles V</u> (1500–1558):** Holy Roman Emperor whose Habsburg territories intensified the conflict with France after his election in 1519.
- **Military Revolution:** Major changes in European warfare involving gunpowder, tactics, fortifications, and army organization.
- **Adoption of firearms:**
  - France led in artillery development but was slower to adopt handguns.
  - Spain became a leader in the use of the arquebus.
- **<u>Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba</u>:** Spanish commander who developed military tactics influenced by classical studies.
- **Arquebus:** An early firearm used by infantry.
- **<u>Spanish Tercio</u>:** Developed by Spain by 1532.
  - Formation of approximately 3,000 men.
  - Combined pikemen and arquebusiers.
  - Integrated firearms with traditional melee weapons to create a more coordinated fighting force.

## Discussion Readings

- **<u>Baldassare Castiglione</u>, “The Ideal of the Gentleman Soldier” (pp. 23–28):** Explores the qualities and conduct expected of an ideal Renaissance gentleman.
- **<u>Niccolò Machiavelli</u>, “A Call for a Renaissance of Military Virtue” and “The Republican Citizen Soldier” (pp. 29–35):** Emphasizes military preparedness, civic responsibility, and the importance of citizens defending their state.

## Summary
Warfare in late medieval Italy changed as wealthy city-states challenged feudal powers and hired professional mercenaries. The crossbow weakened the dominance of armoured knights, while gunpowder artillery made older fortifications increasingly ineffective. The <u>French Invasion of Italy</u> in 1494 demonstrated the advantages of centralized monarchies over the divided Italian city-states. Meanwhile, <u>Renaissance Humanism</u> and the ideas of <u>Niccolò Machiavelli</u> influenced military and political thought. New artillery-resistant fortifications and formations such as the <u>Spanish Tercio</u> reflected the experimentation that helped transform European warfare.
